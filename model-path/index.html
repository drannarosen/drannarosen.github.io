How we model a star cluster

My group builds star clusters inside a computer — from the ground up — so we can ask what their light could ever tell us. Here is that path, one honest step at a time. Nothing below assumes you've seen it before.

  1. 01Initial conditionsYou can't study a cluster until you can build one.
  2. 02DynamicsStars pull on each other. Gravity moves them.
  3. 03ObservablesWe never see masses — only light.
  4. 04InferenceRun it backwards: what could the light ever tell us?

Step 01 · Initial conditions

First, make a cluster

A star cluster is just a crowd of stars that formed together. To model one, the first question is deceptively simple: how many stars, how heavy is each, and where do they sit?

Nature answers the "how heavy" part with a remarkably consistent rule called the initial mass function (IMF): it makesmany small stars and only a rare few massive ones. Below, each star is drawn from that rule — a broken power law measured across real clusters. Resample it, and watch: the crowd is always mostly faint red stars, with a scarce handful of brilliant blue giants. Those giants are rare, but they run the show — a theme you'll meet again in every step that follows.

The masses come from the Kroupa (2001) IMF; each star's color follows its mass through a main-sequence temperature relation. Same code that draws the cluster on the homepage — here it's yours to poke.

Coming next

Then: let it evolve

With a cluster in hand, the next steps set it in motion — gravity pulls the stars into orbits and ejects some entirely; the light we'd actually observe is projected onto the sky and blurred by noise; and finally we run the whole chain backwards to ask what that light could ever have told us about how the cluster was born. Those steps are on the way.
