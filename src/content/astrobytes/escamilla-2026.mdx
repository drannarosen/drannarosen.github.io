Alex Escamilla led this paper with Mike Grudić and me, as an undergraduate in
my group at SDSU; Alex is now an Astronomy Ph.D. student at UMass Amherst. We
took a STARFORGE simulation of a star-forming cloud, observed it the way a
far-infrared survey observes a real one, and asked how much of what observers
measure is the cloud and how much is the measurement.

## Clouds make stars slowly

Stars form in giant molecular clouds: cold, dense clouds of gas, mostly
molecular hydrogen, with a little dust mixed in. Gravity pulls the densest
pockets of gas inward until they collapse into protostars, which keep growing
by drawing in gas from their surroundings.

With gravity alone, a cloud would collapse on its free-fall time $t_{\rm ff}$,
the time it takes to fall in on itself if nothing pushes back; denser gas
collapses faster. However, astronomers find that clouds are inefficient: they
turn only about 1% of their gas into stars per free-fall time. The standard
measure is the star formation efficiency per free-fall time,

$$
\epsilon_{\rm ff} = \frac{\dot{M}_\star}{M_{\rm gas}/t_{\rm ff}},
$$

the fraction of a cloud's gas mass $M_{\rm gas}$ turned into stars in one
gravitational free-fall time $t_{\rm ff}$, given a star formation rate
$\dot{M}_\star$.

Something must slow the collapse. The candidates are turbulence, magnetic
fields, and stellar feedback, the energy and momentum young stars inject into
their surroundings. But none of the three quantities in $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$
can be measured directly. Every observed value is an inference, so the
measurement itself needs scrutiny.

## The observer's recipe

We followed the method
[Pokhrel et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/abf564) applied to
nearby Milky Way clouds. Dust glows in the far infrared; fitting that emission
gives the dust column, and an assumed dust-to-gas ratio converts it to a gas
surface density $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$. Contours at a series of $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$
thresholds define the regions to measure.

The star formation rate comes from counting the youngest protostars, Class 0
young stellar objects (YSOs), inside each contour and assuming they all share
one mass and one lifetime:

$$
\dot{M}_\star = \frac{N_{\rm YSOs}\,\langle M_{\rm YSO}\rangle}{\langle t_{\rm YSO}\rangle},
$$

with $\langle M_{\rm YSO}\rangle = 0.5\,M_\odot$ and a Class 0 lifetime of
$0.5$ Myr. The free-fall time treats the gas inside the contour as a uniform
sphere.

In a real cloud there is no way to check which of these assumptions bias the
result. In a simulation there is.

## Observing a simulation

We used the fiducial STARFORGE run, built on the GIZMO magnetohydrodynamics
code: a turbulent, magnetized cloud of $2 \times 10^4\,M_\odot$ and radius
$\approx 10$ pc that collapses into a star cluster under feedback from
radiation, collimated outflows and stellar winds. It resolves the mass function
of stellar systems down to $0.1\,M_\odot$, and feedback disrupts the cloud at
$\approx 10$ Myr, about 2.5 free-fall times.

With the meshoid analysis package, we turned the simulated gas density and
dust temperature into synthetic dust emission maps at 150, 250, 350 and
500 μm, the bands of the SPIRE instrument on the Herschel Space Observatory,
and ran the observer's recipe on them with the same 1% dust-to-gas ratio. This
time we know the answer: every protostar's true formation time and accretion
history, and the true three-dimensional gas distribution.

## Low efficiencies that change with time

<PostFigure id="escamilla-2026-fig3" variant="wide">
  $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ against $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$ for the simulated cloud over $\sim$10 Myr; each track is the binned median of one snapshot, colored from early (purple) to late (yellow). The gray band is the $1\sigma$ scatter of the Milky Way clouds in Pokhrel et al. (2021), with their median as a solid line.
</PostFigure>

Measured this way, the simulated cloud's efficiency spans 0.05% to 10% across
surface density and time, mostly 1% to 3%, in agreement with observed clouds.
Feedback-regulated star formation produces those low values without extreme
initial conditions.

The efficiency also changes as the cloud evolves. For the first 3–5 Myr,
$\epsilon_{\rm ff} \approx 0.3$–$0.5\%$ at low $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$ and rises
steeply toward higher densities. Later, at fixed thresholds, it rises and falls
in episodes, reaching $\approx 5\%$. The variation within this one cloud
mirrors the variation seen across surveys of many clouds.

Clouds in a survey are caught at different ages, so much of the observed
scatter may reflect evolutionary stage rather than real differences in how
clouds form stars.

## Where the recipe goes wrong

Because the simulation knows the true star formation rate and the true
free-fall time of every gas element, we could swap halves of the recipe. The
paper compares four definitions: fully observational, fully simulation-based,
and two hybrids that take the star formation rate from one and the free-fall
time from the other.

<PostFigure id="escamilla-2026-fig8" variant="wide">
  $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ against $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$ for the four definitions; shaded regions are the $1\sigma$ scatter. The observational form (red) sits above the simulation values (blue) at low $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$ and below them at high $\Sigma_{\rm gas}$; the two cross near $\log\Sigma_{\rm gas} \approx 2.3$ ($M_\odot\,{\rm pc}^{-2}$). The hybrids (green, purple) show which assumption drives each offset.
</PostFigure>

The observed and true values cross near $\log\Sigma_{\rm gas} \approx 2.3$
($\Sigma_{\rm gas}$ in $M_\odot\,{\rm pc}^{-2}$). Below the crossing, the
observer's method gives higher efficiencies, mainly through the star formation
rate: where few stars form, the population is too sparse to sample the mass
function, and a fixed mean mass of $0.5\,M_\odot$ is hard to justify.

Above the crossing, the method gives lower efficiencies. Treating the gas as a
smooth sphere spreads out dense structure, which lengthens the inferred
free-fall time and raises the enclosed gas mass; the second effect wins and
pushes $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ down. Massive protostars break both fixed
assumptions at once: they accrete quickly and spend less time as protostars,
so counting them underestimates the star formation rate exactly where massive
stars form.

The crossing point is set by the method and the resolution, and it moves when
they change.

## Of the biases tested, resolution matters most

<aside class="sidenote">
  <strong>dex</strong>
  A difference in a base-10 logarithm. A change of 1 dex is a factor of ten.
</aside>

We repeated the measurement at three map resolutions: 0.01 pc, the
simulation's own, and 0.04 and 0.08 pc, the last matching Herschel/SPIRE
observations of nearby clouds. Coarser maps blur the compact gas around
protostars, and the inferred efficiency drops, by up to 0.3 dex between 0.08
and 0.01 pc.

Contamination matters less. Background active galactic nuclei (AGN) can pass
for young stellar objects in a protostar catalog, so we injected them at 9 per
square degree, the rate adopted from Pokhrel et al. (2020). They raised
$\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ by up to $\sim 0.1$ dex, only for nearby clouds
(0.1–0.4 kpc) and only at low surface density; by 1 kpc the effect is
negligible.

## What this does not show

This is one simulated cloud with idealized initial conditions: a uniform
sphere of fixed mass, radius, metallicity and magnetic field, whose turbulence
decays instead of being driven, unlike clouds in the Milky Way. The radiative
transfer uses approximations that can underestimate radiation pressure in
complex geometries, and the synthetic maps assume optically thin dust emission.

The protostars were not identified from the synthetic images. They are the
simulation's sink particles, labeled Class 0 by their recorded age, which skips
the misclassification a real pipeline would add. The paper expects a full
observational identification to add scatter without removing the two main
trends, the time dependence and the resolution dependence, but that is not
tested here. Each sink particle may also stand for a single star or an
unresolved multiple system.

So an observed $\epsilon_{\rm ff}$ is a snapshot of a quantity that changes
over a cloud's life, read through a method with its own biases. Stellar
feedback sets the efficiency; the method and the resolution set the number we
read off.
